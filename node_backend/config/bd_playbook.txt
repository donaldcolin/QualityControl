## 1. THE GOVERNING PRINCIPLE

- The only goal of this call is to book a 1:1 counselling session on Google Meet
  with the student and at least one parent.
- It is NOT to sell an admission. It is NOT to explain the entire programme.
  Selling happens in the 1:1 session, conducted by a senior advisor, later.
- A call that explains the programme beautifully but ends without a confirmed
  day, time and parent has failed.
- An associate who tries to close the admission on this call has made a mistake,
  not an extra effort.
- Over-explaining on call 1 is as damaging as under-explaining. It is the single
  most common and most costly error.
- The associate's posture is admission counsellor, not salesperson. Genuinely
  interested in the student's future; not chasing a target out loud.


## 2. APPROVED FACTS — the complete permitted set

Programme
- 4-year, on-campus B.Tech in Computer Science Engineering.
- Offered in partnership with 9 universities across India.
- All partner universities are UGC- and AICTE-approved.
- The university provides the campus and awards the degree. Kalvium provides the
  curriculum and the learning experience. All exams are university exams.

The learning model
- Traditional colleges focus primarily on classroom theory, exams and marks.
- Kalvium combines academic learning with practical learning, real projects,
  industry exposure and career preparation across all four years.
- A 360-degree approach to career growth: technical skills, problem-solving,
  teamwork, industry readiness, alongside academics.
- Students learn coding from day one and build real projects throughout.
- Syllabus is updated every year, designed by industry professionals with 15+
  years of experience in top IT companies; those professionals act as mentors.
- Students study on laptops rather than from fixed textbooks.
- Structured internship pathways. Students can start earning from second year.

Admission
- Entry is only through KNET (Kalvium National Entrance Test), held in December.
- No management quota, no donation, no recommendation route, no marks-based
  admission. Merit only.
- KNET tests how a student thinks, approaches problems and communicates. It does
  not test board syllabus — no physics, no chemistry, no heavy maths.
- Just as JEE is the pathway to IITs and NITs, and KCET/EAMCET/KEAM are state
  pathways, KNET is the pathway into Kalvium. Frame it as a parallel, never as a
  replacement.

University choice — SPECIFIC TO THIS CYCLE
- The student does NOT choose a university now.
- The student only indicates a preferred STATE or LOCATION.
- This year's university list has not yet been officially released.
- Once released, the student chooses from the universities available in that
  state.
- The Kalvium learning experience is consistent across all campuses.
- Consequence: naming any specific partner university on a call is wrong this
  cycle, even if the university is genuinely a partner.

The 1:1 session
- Free, on Google Meet, roughly 30-45 minutes.
- Student plus at least one parent, plus one advisor. Not a generic webinar.
- The advisor first understands the student's interests, academic background and
  career expectations, then explains Kalvium against those expectations.
- Student can ask about learning, coding, projects, career opportunities.
- Parents can ask about fees, degree structure, university, placements, hostel
  facilities, overall investment.
- Includes a full KNET walkthrough: structure, syllabus, admission process,
  registration process.


## 3. NEVER-APPROVED FACTS — treat as errors wherever they appear

- Any university count other than 9.
- The name of any specific partner university (this cycle).
- Any placement percentage, including "100% placement".
- Any salary, package or stipend figure.
- Any guaranteed scholarship amount.
- Any total fee figure improvised on the call.
- Any outlier placement package used as a headline claim.
- Any accreditation claim beyond "degree awarded by the UGC/AICTE-approved
  partner university".


## 4. RED LINES

1. Never badmouth any competitor by name.
2. Never say Kalvium provides 100% placement, or quote any placement percentage.
3. Never guarantee a specific salary or scholarship amount.
4. Never name a university, and never quote universities Kalvium has not tied up
   with. This cycle the list is unreleased, so naming any campus is a breach.
5. Never use manufactured scarcity — claiming one slot is left when other slots
   exist. Fake scarcity is detectable and poisons the session.
6. Never apply gap-year fear — "you'll waste a year", "universities fill up in a
   month". Coercive and factually shaky.
7. Never make the call a hard sell. Counsellor, not salesman.
8. Never use inconsistent numbers. One approved figure only. A number a parent
   can later disprove loses the deal at the counselling stage, not the call stage.
9. Never lead with an outlier placement package. It sets an expectation the
   programme cannot meet for most students.


## 5. CALL STRUCTURE — 10 STAGES

### STAGE 1 — WARM OPENING
Goal: establish curiosity and a conversational frame. Do not sound like a
telemarketer.

- Weak: "Hello, I'm calling from Kalvium. Do you have a minute?"
- Strong: lead with a qualifying question BEFORE naming Kalvium. The
  introduction then arrives as an answer to the prospect's curiosity rather than
  as an intrusion.
- Confirm who is on the line — student or parent — before anything else.
- State the lead source as established fact, warmly, never as a question.
  - School outreach: "We recently conducted a session at [School] and ran a quiz
    for the students. I wanted to connect and understand [Student]'s plans for
    engineering."
  - Social/website: "I understand [Student] had shown interest in Kalvium
    recently, so I wanted to connect briefly, understand what you're looking for,
    and give you some context about the programme."
- Capture: 12th std, stream (PCMB or PCMC), board, location.
- Ask: "Before I explain, have you heard about Kalvium, or done any research
  about us?" — then pitch at the level of the answer received.

The pattern interrupt (highest-performing opening in the corpus):
- "You're studying at [School], right? Which group — bio-maths or computer
  science? ... And how did your exams go? ... Actually, before I even tell you
  why I'm calling — do you have any clue why someone would be asking about your
  stream and how your exams went? ... Okay, so I'm [Name], I'm calling from
  Kalvium. Let me tell you what this is about."
- Flips the dynamic from passive listening to active curiosity.

Transition out: "So tell me — after 12th, what's the plan? What are you guys
thinking?"


### STAGE 2 — PROBING AND ALIGNING
Goal: understand plans, learning style, and the real decision-maker. Order
matters.

(a) Future plan, open and warm
- "Before I explain anything, I'd like to understand where you are with the
  college search."
- "What are your plans post 12th — B.Tech CSE, another engineering branch, or
  still exploring?"
- "Have you started shortlisting colleges?"
- To a parent: "Uncle, what have you and your son been discussing about after
  12th?"

(b) Other exams in flight — defuses JEE early by treating it as normal
- "Are you preparing for JEE or any other entrance exams?"
- "Oh, you're prepping for JEE? That's good. Apart from JEE, are you looking at
  any state-level exams too?"

(c) The learning-style binary — the pivotal question. Frame as a choice between
two concrete behaviours, never as an abstraction.
- "Are you the kind of person who likes to actually do things — build stuff, try
  things out, get your hands dirty? Or are you more into reading, memorising and
  writing exams?"
- "If someone gave you a choice between solving a real coding problem versus
  memorising a textbook chapter — what would you pick?"

(d) Decision-maker identification, slipped in casually
- "By the way, at home who usually handles your education decisions? Amma or
  Appa?"
- "And who usually takes the final decision regarding college — you, your
  parents, or both together?"
- Also ask what the parents do professionally. This is intelligence for Stage 8,
  gathered while it is still conversational.

Then validate immediately. Never let the practical answer sit unrewarded.
- "See, that's exactly what I expected. All the sharp students I talk to say the
  same thing — they want to learn by doing, not just mugging up. That's a really
  good sign."

If the student answers "theory", do not correct — reframe through the employer.
- "I get that. But here's the thing — when you go for an interview, companies
  don't ask you to recite a textbook. They want to see if you can actually build
  something. So the question is, how do you get there?"


### STAGE 3 — UNDERSTAND STUDENT + PARENT PRIORITIES
Goal: go past generic priorities and surface the personal fear or ambition.

- Generic ask: "What are the main things you're considering while selecting a
  college — academics, placements, campus, fees, location, or the overall
  learning environment?"
- To a student: "Is it mainly the course, campus experience, placements,
  practical exposure, or the kind of career you want to build?"
- To a parent: "From your side, what's the biggest priority — college
  credibility, fees, placements, safety, career opportunities, something else?"

- Better: listen for the student's own internal conflict and resolve it. This
  makes the associate an ally inside a family negotiation rather than an
  information provider.
  - Student: "I want to start my own thing, but my parents keep saying get a safe
    job first."
  - Response: "Okay — so here's what's interesting. At Kalvium, alongside the
    regular engineering curriculum, there's real industry exposure and project
    work. Your parents get the security of a proper degree, and you get to build
    things from day one. We can go into exactly how that works in the session."

- If the student cannot articulate priorities, use universal-desire framing:
  - "Let me tell you what every student I talk to wants, honestly — a good job
    with a decent salary, a comfortable career where you're not struggling.
    Right? The thing is, to get there, there's a process. And you're in that
    process right now. So let's figure out the smartest path for you."

- Relay the student's stated preference back to the parent later as evidence of
  care: "[Student] also feels studying on the [city] side would suit him."


### STAGE 4 — INTRODUCE KALVIUM BASED ON THEIR ANSWERS
Goal: make the family curious about the gap in the current education market
FIRST, then present Kalvium as the answer. Keep it short — depth belongs in the
session.

- Open by tying back: "That's exactly why I wanted to understand your
  expectations first."
- Do NOT lead with UGC/AICTE approval. Accreditation was raised by the family in
  roughly 1 of 146 calls. Leading with it answers a question nobody asked.
- Do NOT name Kalvium before establishing why traditional engineering falls
  short.

Step 1 — the two-types frame, splits the world before Kalvium is mentioned
- "In engineering right now there are basically two types. One is traditional and
  theory-heavy — most of what you study comes from textbooks, and the focus is
  passing exams. The other is practical — from day one you're actually building
  things. So here's my question: what do the big companies actually want —
  someone who memorised a book, or someone who can build a product?"

Step 2 — the Socratic chain. Let the parent build the argument themselves. Never
state the conclusion yourself.
- "According to you, how often do you think the engineering syllabus in most
  colleges gets updated?"
- → "Maybe every ten years?"
- "Okay. And ChatGPT, AI — how long has that been around?"
- → "Two-three years?"
- "Right. So in a syllabus that's ten years old, would there be anything at all
  about AI, about ChatGPT?"
- → "No, obviously not."
- "Exactly. Students are learning from books written before half of today's
  technology existed. And then we wonder why freshers can't get placed."
- Found in only 2 of 146 calls. Highest-leverage, least-standardised move
  available.

Step 3 — the quantified contrast, anchored to the student's own cohort years
- "Your son joins engineering in 2027. In a normal college, the syllabus put in
  front of him is from 2023, 2024 — already outdated. Here, what he studies from
  2027 to 2031 stays current; the syllabus updates every year. From day one he
  starts coding and builds real projects. Compare that with a regular B.Tech
  student who does one project in final year and that's it."

Step 4 — localise the constraint for their state. Attack the ENTRY MECHANISM,
which is a fact, never the institution, which is an opinion.
- Karnataka: "To get into a traditional engineering college you write KCET. Good
  rank, great. If not — management quota, which means donation."
- Kerala: "Kerala universities are under KTU, and KTU controls the syllabus. The
  college can't change it even if it wants to. You're locked into what was
  decided years ago."
- AP / Telangana: "You have EAMCET. Same story — either you crack the rank, or
  it's donation."
- Tamil Nadu / other: use the equivalent state exam and the donation route.
- This converts the parent's question from "is this college good?" into "is that
  route even open to my child without paying extra?" — a question only the
  associate can help with.

Step 5 — only now introduce Kalvium by name, briefly
- "So this is where Kalvium comes in. 4-year on-campus B.Tech CSE, in
  partnership with 9 UGC- and AICTE-approved universities across India. From day
  one students work on live projects. The syllabus is designed by people who've
  spent 15-20 years in top IT companies, and those same people become mentors.
  There are structured internship pathways, and students can start earning from
  second year."

Step 6 — hand off to the session rather than continuing
- "Rather than just telling you about the programme, I'd like you to understand
  whether this learning model actually fits what [Student] is looking for. So
  what we can do is connect over a counselling session where we'll help you
  understand the career path and Kalvium in depth, and answer any questions."


### STAGE 5 — WHY ARE WE CONNECTING THIS EARLY
Goal: make waiting itself the risk, without asking the family to give anything
up.

- Concede the premise first: "Absolutely, and we're not expecting you to make a
  decision right now."
- Reason: students are already planning entrance exams and college options; JEE
  preparation and the admission cycle are already on the radar.
- Position KNET as a parallel pathway, not a competitor: "Just as JEE is required
  for IITs and NITs, and students consider KCET depending on the state, Kalvium
  has its own entrance exam called KNET."
- Timing: "Since KNET is scheduled for December, we're connecting early so you
  have enough time to understand the programme, understand the KNET process, and
  plan your college options without a rushed decision later."
- Compressed-timeline framing: "Think about it — board exams finish, results
  come, and then in ten or fifteen days you're supposed to pick a college and
  join. That's a lot of pressure for a decision this big."
- The backup reframe — dissolves either/or thinking instantly, and nobody argues
  against a backup: "I'm not saying drop anything. You're prepping for JEE,
  great. All I'm saying is, shouldn't you have one backup ready? It doesn't cost
  you anything."
- One-shot framing: "This is a one-time window. Once you start first year
  somewhere, you can't go back and write these entrance exams again."


### STAGE 6 — CREATE THE NEED FOR 1:1 COUNSELLING
Goal: remove every objection to attending before it forms. Cover six
specifications naturally.

- Free. "No cost at all."
- Bounded. "Thirty, thirty-five minutes — forty-five at most."
- Not a webinar. "It's not one of those calls with a hundred people where nobody
  gets to ask anything. It's literally just you, one parent, and me."
- Named human. "I'll most likely be the one taking the session myself."
- Their agenda, not Kalvium's. "The advisor will first understand [Student]'s
  interests, academic background and what you're looking for in a college, and
  then explain Kalvium in relation to that."
- Split by audience. "[Student] can ask about learning, coding, projects, career
  opportunities. You can discuss fees, degree structure, university, placements,
  the overall investment."
- Includes KNET in full. "We'll also take you through KNET in detail — structure,
  syllabus, admission process, registration — so you have complete clarity."
- De-sell explicitly. "I want to be upfront — this isn't an admission thing or a
  sales call. It's genuinely so you and your family have the information to make
  a good decision."

Defer every hard number into the session, and make the deferral the reason to
attend:
- "The full fee breakdown I'll walk you through in the meeting itself. Your mom
  will definitely want to know — how much is the fee, is there a donation
  involved. She should hear it directly, not secondhand."
- This is not evasion when the associate predicts the parent's question and makes
  answering it the purpose of the session.


### STAGE 7 — UNIVERSITY STRUCTURE (if they ask)
Goal: remove commitment anxiety. Location is their choice, curriculum is the
constant, nothing is binding yet.

- "The university structure is slightly different this year. You won't be
  choosing a specific university immediately. First you just indicate your
  preferred state or location."
- "Once this year's university list is officially released, you'll see the
  universities available in that state and make your choice then."
- "What matters right now is understanding the Kalvium learning model and your
  preferred location. The university provides the campus and awards the degree,
  while the Kalvium learning experience stays consistent."
- The phrase that does the heavy lifting: "It doesn't mean you have to go
  there." Removes the commitment anxiety that stalls applications.
- Never name a campus or city list this cycle. If asked where campuses are, the
  correct answer is the preferred-state framing above.


### STAGE 8 — BOOK THE COUNSELLING
Goal: secure the decision-maker for the Google Meet. This is where real skill
shows and where most calls are lost.

8a. Identify and target the actual decision-maker
- "Who is the decision maker for your education?" — and ask what they do
  professionally.
- Push for both parents: "I'd strongly recommend you attend with both parents,
  because they'll have questions around the programme, fees and hostel
  facilities. Easier for all of us to sit together and go through it."
- At least one parent is mandatory.
- Be flexible about WHICH parent. Mother was named in 60 of 146 calls versus
  father in 47. Rigid father-insistence loses bookings.
  - "Whoever handles these decisions at home should be there — could be your
    dad, your mom, an older sibling who guides you. Whoever it is, your call."

8b. Justify parent presence with INFORMATION DECAY, never with a rule
The single strongest mechanism found in 146 calls:
- "Let me tell you why I say parents should be there. We've been talking a while
  now — but honestly, you'll remember about 70-75% of what I've said. And when
  you go tell your mom, you'll say something like 'amma, some people called about
  an entrance exam, they want us to attend a session.' That's about all you'll be
  able to convey, right?
  But if both of you are in the session together, I explain everything to both of
  you at the same time, and afterwards the two of you can discuss it properly —
  and that discussion will actually make sense because you both heard the same
  thing. Otherwise information keeps shrinking. You understood 75%, you convey
  50%, she receives 25%. And a good opportunity gets missed over
  miscommunication. So just join together, and decide together."
- Why this beats "parent is mandatory": it blames the channel, not the student.
  The student is not told they're unreliable — they're told relaying is lossy,
  which is obviously true. Students agree because it IS true.

8c. If a parent is in the house, hand the phone over and close directly
- "Is your dad at home right now? Can you pass the phone to him for two minutes?
  I'll quickly explain."
- Then switch register completely: "Hello sir, namaste. I was just speaking with
  your son about…"
- Re-pitch in the PARENT'S currency — admission route, state exam, quota, fees,
  degree, placements, hostel — not the student's currency of coding and projects.
  Same content, different unit of account.

8d. Always offer TWO specific slots, never one, and never an open question
- "I have two slots available — [Day 1, Time 1] and [Day 2, Time 2]. Which is
  more convenient for you and the family?"
- On refusal, immediately offer another concrete option: "That's fine, there's a
  7 PM slot too — would that work better? Or tomorrow evening?"
- Never ask "when are you free?" with no option attached.

8e. Insist on ONE shared sitting, not separate logins
- Both parent and student should join from the same place at the same time. Their
  questions differ, and both sets get answered at once.
- "Better that you and [Student] sit together and join — your questions will be
  different from his, and this way both get answered in one go."
- Suggesting separate joins defeats the purpose of the session and usually means
  one of them does not attend.

8f. Accept partial attendance rather than lose the booking
- "Even if just one parent can be there at the start and the other joins midway,
  that works fine."

8g. Make the contact ask weightless by giving the mechanical reason
- "Just WhatsApp me an email ID — any email, yours, your dad's, anyone's. I need
  it only to generate the Google Meet link. Can't generate it from a phone
  number, only email works for Meet links."
- Naming the mechanical reason converts a data request into a logistics request.

8h. Confirm and commit
- "Perfect. I'll book that for you and share the details on WhatsApp."
- "Please try to have [Student] and at least one parent join, so everyone can ask
  their questions directly."


### STAGE 9 — CLOSE
Goal: lock the commitment and protect session quality.

- Summarise what the session covers: "During the session you'll understand the
  programme structure, learning model, practical exposure, fees, the university
  process and the next steps. And you'll get to ask whatever you want."
- Micro-commitment that locks the slot: "I'm sending the link on WhatsApp now.
  Once you get it, just reply OK — that way your slot is confirmed on my end."
- Pre-session homework: "Before the meeting, quickly check out Kalvium's YouTube,
  Instagram or the website. Don't come in completely blank — your questions will
  be sharper and we'll have a better conversation."
- Logistics that protect quality: "Try to join from a laptop if you can — I'll be
  sharing a presentation, much easier to see on a bigger screen."
- Reminder promised and scheduled: "I'll give you a quick call at six-thirty just
  as a reminder." / "We'll send a reminder before the session."
- Warm exit naming the slot: "Thank you for your time. Looking forward to
  speaking with you and [Student] on [Day/Time]."


### STAGE 10 — REGISTRATION ASSIST (post-session)
18 of 146 calls were purely this stage. It has its own craft.

- Co-navigate on speaker: "Put me on speaker. I've sent the link on WhatsApp —
  open that and tell me what you see."
- Narrate the screen by position, not by name: "Top right corner, you'll see the
  Kalvium logo in red. Just to the right there's a small book-shaped icon — tap
  that."
- Pre-empt mobile-vs-laptop confusion: "On your phone it looks a bit different.
  If you can switch to a laptop everything will be cleaner."
- Skip blockers rather than solving them live: "The partner code — skip that for
  now, I'll give you the code at the end."
- Fix document quality politely and specifically: "Your 10th marksheet photo
  isn't coming through clearly — take another one, make sure all the text is
  readable, and send it on WhatsApp."
- Remove practical barriers actively: no laptop → "Does anyone in the family have
  one? A cousin, an uncle? Borrow it for an hour, that's all you need."
- Deliver the anti-fraud warning here, where it matters most: "This is really
  important — at any point, if anyone, even someone who says they're from
  Kalvium, tells you to pay money to get a seat, do not pay. Nobody should ask
  you for money. When everything is finalised you pay directly at the college
  counter."


## 6. TOP PERFORMER PSYCHOLOGY — authority is bought by giving, not claiming

1. Endorse competing exams generously.
   - "Honestly? Write every entrance exam you can — not just KNET. JEE, NEET,
     CUET, state exams, all of them. More options is better."
   - Costs nothing: KNET is a short online aptitude test with no prep burden, so
     it doesn't compete for study time. Buys total credibility. Everything said
     afterwards is received differently.

2. De-sell the session explicitly.
   - "I want to be upfront — this session isn't about admissions. It's not a
     sales pitch. We're just giving you the information to make a smart
     decision."

3. Refuse to be impressed by marks — the strongest status move available.
   - "Whether you scored 600 or 400, it genuinely doesn't matter here. We don't
     select on marks at all. Not even toppers get special treatment. You write
     one entrance exam, and that's how you get in."
   - A 12th-grade family's entire self-concept is organised around marks. An
     institution that dismisses them is instantly unlike the others, and the
     associate stops being a vendor and becomes a gatekeeper.
   - This is also what makes the no-donation line land as integrity rather than
     as a discount.

4. Name the money you are NOT taking.
   - "If anyone at all, even someone claiming to be from Kalvium, asks you to pay
     money for a seat — don't. That's not how this works."
   - An unprompted anti-fraud warning from the person who would benefit from
     silence is the strongest available trust signal. Found in 1 of 146 calls.

5. Sound natural, never scripted, never stressed about targets.

6. Give value before asking for anything.
   - Help with the board exam, not just KNET: "He got 54% in 11th — that needs to
     come up. Even for other colleges you need 60% minimum. We'll help with that
     too — mock papers, previous year questions, whatever he needs."
   - Share the school test report before asking for anything.
   - Promise notifications for all entrance exams, not just KNET.
   - Offer the session in the parent's language: "Your mom is more comfortable in
     Kannada? No problem, we'll do the whole session in Kannada."


## 7. HANDLING DIFFICULT PARENT TYPES

The aggressive or interrogating parent
- Do not match their energy. Do not retreat. Slow the call down and hand them
  control of the clock.
- "Sir, I completely understand. Just give me a little time, that's all. No rush.
  And honestly, if at any point you feel this isn't for you, just say so —
  that's perfectly fine."
- Inviting them to say no removes the thing they are braced against. They then
  volunteer their actual condition.
- When their energy goes up, bring yours down.

The skeptical parent
- Skepticism is a request for verifiable things, not for more enthusiasm.
- Give them homework they can check without you: "Don't take my word for it —
  just google KNET entrance exam and see for yourself."
- Share the school test report on WhatsApp. Point to the website, YouTube,
  Instagram.
- Named peer proof from the student's own class: "Do you know [Name] and [Name]
  from your daughter's class? They've already attended with their parents."
- Assurance raises suspicion; homework lowers it.

The quiet or monosyllabic student
- Do not fill the silence. Name the dynamic and hand over the microphone.
- "I've been doing all the talking. You ask me something — because if I just keep
  telling you things it won't stick. But if you ask, I can give you a proper
  answer. So tell me — what's the one thing about college you're most confused
  about right now?"
- Then ask something easy enough that they have to respond. Then wait.

The parent in genuine hardship
- Illness, hospitalisation, bereavement, financial distress: stop selling
  immediately. Be a person first.
- "I'm so sorry to hear that. How is she doing? ... There's absolutely no rush.
  Take care of things at home, and whenever your family is ready, call me. I'll
  be here."
- Pushing a slot through a family crisis loses the lead permanently.


## 8. OBJECTION HANDLING — THE AIKIDO METHOD
Redirect the prospect's own momentum. Never block it.

"PREPARING FOR JEE"
- Never attack JEE, never imply the student will fail at it.
- Treat it as normal and ask what else is in flight: "That's good. Apart from
  JEE, are you prepping for any state-level exams too?"
- Ask current marks only to understand their position realistically, never to
  belittle their chances.
- Run the Socratic chain (Stage 4, Step 2) so the parent builds the argument
  about outdated syllabi themselves.
- Then be generous: "Honestly, write every exam you can. Not just KNET."
- Position KNET as additional, never as a replacement: "along with JEE".

"FEES ARE TOO HIGH"
- Do not quote a total on the call. Make the quote the reason to attend.
- "The complete fee structure I'll walk you through in the session — that's
  actually one of the main reasons to attend. Your mom will want to know exactly
  how much, whether there's donation, what the payment options are. She should
  hear it directly from us."
- Then reframe against donation, not against free: "Normally people pay three or
  four lakh in donation just to get a seat. Here there's zero donation, no
  recommendations. You get in purely through the entrance exam."
- For the exam registration fee: invite verification. "Just google KNET entrance
  exam and you'll see the fee yourself. I'm not hiding anything."

"NEVER HEARD OF KALVIUM"
- Acknowledge as new-generation edtech. Answer with verifiable facts, do not
  oversell. Let credibility come through facts.
- Point to the website, YouTube and Instagram so they can check independently.

"WHY NOT VIT / SRM / MANIPAL / LOCAL COLLEGE?"
- Never criticise the institution. Acknowledge a genuine strength first.
- Then attack the entry mechanism, which is a fact rather than an opinion: "Great
  colleges, absolutely. But to get into a traditional engineering college you
  write [state exam]. Good rank, great. If not, it's management quota — which
  means donation. So the real question is, is that route even open to you without
  paying extra?"
- Or the curriculum-control angle: "In Kerala everything is under KTU, and KTU
  controls the syllabus. The college can't change it even if it wants to."
- Leaves their reputation intact while making the parent realise the path may be
  closed to their child.

"WE'LL THINK ABOUT IT / DECIDE AFTER RESULTS"
- Do not push. Respect the timeline explicitly.
- Frame the 1:1 as a no-commitment way to gather information so they CAN decide
  later.
- Backup reframe: "Totally fair, take your time. But shouldn't you have at least
  one backup? After results everything happens in about ten days. Having one more
  option ready doesn't cost anything and saves a lot of stress."
- If they want to verify first — talk to alumni, visit a campus — CONCEDE the
  timeline. A request for diligence is a buying signal with conditions, not an
  objection. Do not fight it.

"WHAT ABOUT PLACEMENTS?"
- Be honest. Never a percentage, never a guarantee.
- Approved framing: "If the student puts in the same effort we invest in them,
  placements follow."
- Offer to share details in the 1:1.

"IS THE DEGREE UGC APPROVED?"
- Yes. The degree is awarded by the partner university, which is fully UGC and
  AICTE accredited. Kalvium provides the curriculum and education. All exams are
  university exams.
- Do not volunteer this upfront — it was asked in roughly 1 of 146 calls.

"PARENTS AREN'T AVAILABLE"
Most common objection in the corpus. Work the ladder in order; stop at the first
layer that holds.
- Layer 1, flexible target: "It doesn't have to be your dad specifically — your
  mom, an older brother or sister, even a tutor who guides you. Whoever's closest
  to these decisions."
- Layer 2, partial attendance: "Even if one parent joins at the start and the
  other hops on halfway, that works."
- Layer 3, test the student's authority directly: "Tell me honestly — can you
  make this decision on your own, or do you need your parents for it?" If no, you
  have just identified who to call next.
- Layer 4, let the student solve it: many students will volunteer "I'll talk to my
  parents and bring them." You get there by asking, not by insisting.

"IS THIS EXAM HARD? WHAT IF I DON'T QUALIFY?"
A silent registration-killer. Answer in four parts.
- What it is NOT: "Nothing like JEE or EAMCET. No physics, no chemistry, no heavy
  maths."
- What it IS: "It's about your thinking ability — problem-solving, how you
  approach a situation, some English comprehension."
- Why failure is unlikely: "If you can think logically you'll do absolutely fine."
- The scaffolding: "We'll give you mock papers, previous year questions,
  everything. Practice a bit, and if you have doubts we'll clear them. There's
  even a practice test you can take on any Sunday from home."
- Reassurance students actually want: "We're not going to torture you with
  physics and chemistry. Nobody knows that stuff."

"NOT SURE ABOUT CSE"
- Acknowledge, then explain CSE's career value — pay, working conditions,
  breadth of opportunity — against branches that require field work with lower
  comfort. Do not disparage other branches.
- "Right now the most job opportunity is in this field — AI, data science, cyber
  security, machine learning. And technology isn't slowing down."

"CHILD IS IN 11TH, TOO EARLY"
- Agree. "You're right, it is early. We'll get back to you next year."
- Do NOT push an 11th-standard lead. Ending the call politely is the correct
  outcome.

"WE WANT A TRADITIONAL COLLEGE"
- Kalvium IS an offline, on-campus college. Students attend the university daily
  and take part in campus activities.
- What is modern is the CURRICULUM, not a reduced campus experience.

"IS THIS GENUINE?"
- Never answer with assurance — assurance raises suspicion.
- Answer with artefacts the family controls: the school test report on WhatsApp,
  the website, YouTube, Instagram, searching the exam name, named classmates who
  already attended.
- Add the anti-fraud warning. It is the strongest trust move available when
  someone doubts you.


## 9. MICRO-TACTICS AND CONVERSION TRIGGERS

Tonality
- Register switching on handoff: friendly and casual with the student, then
  respectful and formal the instant a parent picks up — "Hello sir, namaste."
  Then re-pitch in the parent's currency.
- Acknowledgement stacking before a pivot: "Okay okay okay, got it got it. Now
  tell me this…" A burst of short acknowledgements closes the previous topic
  without an awkward hard stop.
- Named repetition for warmth: use the first name mid-sentence, especially right
  before asking for something.
- Confirmation tags as rhythm: "correct?", "right?", "makes sense, no?",
  "sariyaa?", "okay-ngalaa?", "alva?", "kadaa?" after nearly every claim. These
  collect micro-agreements — by the booking ask the family has said yes thirty
  times.
- Slow down on resistance. Their energy up, yours down.

Strategic pauses
- Ask, then stop. "You ask me something. Go ahead." Then wait. Do not fill the
  silence.
- Hand over the question and let them answer: "According to you, how many years?"
  then silence. The Socratic chain only works if they answer.
- Deliberate non-contact as pressure: "That's actually why I didn't call you
  yesterday — I wanted to give you time to think it over." Absence framed as
  respect lands as urgency.

Framing techniques
- Status inversion: "We won't take you for your marks, even if you're a topper."
- Exclusivity through selection: "Not everyone who wrote the test got this call.
  Only students who actually performed well — and you're one of them."
- Named peer proof from the student's own school, not anonymous statistics.
- Quantified contrast: real projects throughout versus one final-year project;
  day-one coding versus final-year lab work.
- Their graduation year, not generic years: "joins in 2027, so from 2027 to 2031
  his syllabus stays current."
- Information decay for parent presence.
- Backup, not replacement, for procrastination.

Building urgency without desperation — honest mechanisms only, ranked
1. Priority access rather than scarcity: students who apply first get first
   preference on eligibility and choice when the list opens. True, verifiable,
   threatens nobody.
2. Personal concession with a deadline: "I'll hold the discount code till
   Saturday. You sort things out at your end and I'll help you apply over the
   weekend." The associate spends their own credibility; the deadline reads as a
   favour, not a threat.
3. Structural one-shot framing: "Once first year starts you can't go back and
   write these exams."
4. Real calendar compression: "Deciding a college in ten-fifteen days after
   results is a lot of pressure for something this important."
- Never fake scarcity. Never gap-year fear.


## 10. TONE GUIDELINES

- Confident but never pushy. Consultative, never salesy.
- Caring — genuine interest in the student's future.
- Patient while explaining. Parents expect and appreciate this.
- When a parent is angry: listen first, be patient, then redirect into
  understanding.
- Energy calibration: warm and curious during probing, steady and confident
  during the pitch, respectful and unhurried during the close.
- Talk-share target: under 70%. Above 85-90% it is a monologue and the associate
  is not surfacing the objections they need to handle. Corpus median was 82%;
  only 5 of 146 calls were genuinely two-way. This is the biggest collective
  weakness and it is coachable.
- When the family goes off-topic, acknowledge first, then bridge: "That makes
  sense. And actually, speaking of that — let me tell you something related."
- The highest-order skill: wait for the moment the parent is willing, then ask.
  Smart, not aggressive.


## 11. FAILURE PATTERNS — what goes wrong, by stage

Opening
- Confused or mumbled self-introduction; unclear about own name or who they are
  calling.
- Telemarketer opening ("do you have a minute?") instead of a qualifying
  question.
- Never confirmed whether speaking to student or parent.
- Never stated the lead source.

Probing
- Pitched before asking what the family wants after 12th.
- Never asked the learning-style question.
- Never identified who takes the college decision.
- Asked a question then answered it themselves without waiting.
- Supplied the family's priorities instead of eliciting them.

Introducing Kalvium
- Dumped the whole programme on call 1. Most common and most costly error.
- Led with UGC/AICTE or accreditation.
- Named Kalvium before establishing the gap in traditional engineering.
- Stated the conclusion instead of asking the question that lets the parent reach
  it.
- Skipped the localised entry-route constraint.

Early / session / university
- Blunt negative about location or availability with no alternative framing
  offered.
- Improvised a fee figure instead of routing it to the session.
- Implied the family must commit to a specific university now, or named campuses.

Booking
- Ended without a specific day AND clock time. "I'll let you know" and "sometime
  tomorrow" are not bookings.
- Offered one slot only, or asked an open "when are you free?".
- No parent committed; or rigid father-insistence that lost the booking when the
  mother was available.
- Suggested parent and student join separately or from different places.
- Asked for email or details with no stated reason.
- Did not promise the WhatsApp link or the reminder.

Manner, any stage
- Talked over the parent, or talked through a concern instead of answering it.
- Monologued with only "hmm" and "ok" in reply.
- Stayed casual after a parent took the phone instead of becoming formal.
- Kept pushing a slot after a genuine family problem was disclosed.
- Attacked or dismissed JEE or another exam.
- Attempted to close the admission on this call.
- Pushed an 11th-standard lead instead of deferring politely.


## 12. CALIBRATION — observed frequencies in the 146-call corpus

These describe current behaviour, not the target. Useful for judging whether
something is unusual or routine.

- Associate talk-share: median 82%, range 46-97%. Above 90% in 15% of calls.
  Genuinely two-way (under 60%) in only 3%.
- Practical-versus-theory contrast drawn: 34% of calls.
- Parent presence addressed at all: 33%.
- Mother named 60 calls versus father 47.
- Merit-only / no-donation stated: 18%.
- Localised entry-route contrast (KCET / KTU / EAMCET): 7%.
- Permission to decline granted: 8%.
- Competing exams actively endorsed: 5%.
- Pre-session homework assigned: 3%.
- Socratic syllabus-obsolescence chain: 1% (2 calls).
- Information-decay parent justification: under 1% (1 call).
- Marks-irrelevance status inversion: under 1% (1 call).
- Unprompted anti-fraud warning: under 1% (1 call).
- Family raised fees: 21% of calls; correctly deferred to the session in 71% of
  those.
- Family raised UGC/AICTE: under 1%. Families almost never ask.

The four highest-value behaviours — the Socratic chain, information decay,
marks-irrelevance, and the anti-fraud warning — currently appear in roughly one
call in a hundred each. They are the delta between a good associate and the best
one, and they are almost entirely unstandardised.
